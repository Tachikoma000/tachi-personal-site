Come, let me tell you about the love first,
because before that night there was love.

Bright white light with no opinion of the hour,
the same clean blaze at noon and midnight,
and under it a young engineer, fresh from school,
walking into the hum of a working lab
the way some men walk into a cathedral.
The click and clack of power supplies
changing states like patient soldiers,
the multiplexers ticking through their channels,
tick, tick, tick, a rosary of measurements,
the white noise of the vents keeping their vigil,
the low turning hymn of turbo pumps
pulling the air itself out of the way of the truth.
Physics has a music. I heard it young.
It has never once stopped playing.

Striped wires sorted like families,
red to red, black to black, signal and ground,
ribbon cables braided with more care
than most men comb their sons' hair.
The soldering iron's exquisite little bite,
that bright quick burn on the fingertip,
the apprenticeship fee the craft collects
and every builder gladly pays.
Delicate work judged by brutal machines,
a human hand laying down a joint
that a vacuum will interrogate without mercy,
tedious, repetitive, holy hours,
torque this, seal that, log everything,
because the instrument believes no one,
and is right not to.

Then through the doors to the wet lab,
where the air changes and announces itself,
acid and solvent and that sharp chemical tang
that tells you honest work is underway,
mixing electrolytes like a careful cook,
building cells that either sing or sulk,
and most days they sulked.
Failed run after failed run after failed run,
the long ledger of almost,
and then, some blessed evening, a trace
rising clean on the screen,
one good curve after forty crooked ones,
and the whole year suddenly worth it.
Discovery is ninety parts tedium,
nine parts failure,
one part music,
and the one part pays for all the rest.

And down the corridor, the other church.

The machine shop, the most dangerous room
in that entire building
and somehow the safest place i knew.
The lathe. The mill. The grinders.
The CNC with its patient violence,
the printer laying down its slow ideas.
Everything a young engineer could dream of
standing under fluorescent light,
smelling of cutting oil and hot steel.

Two men ran it, and they were masters.
I will not write their names.
They belong to their own lives now.

I sat on a stool and watched them work
the way a boy watches an uncle mend an engine.
They taught me what no course had taught me.
The eccentricities of a lathe, which has moods.
How to set your base, how to hold your stock,
that speed and heat and cooling and finish
are one argument and not four.
They taught me that a drawing is a wish
and a part is a fact,
and the distance between them
is where engineering actually lives.

We never designed a thing they could not build.
Not once, in all those years.

And they were funny. God, they were funny.
Afternoons with the shop door propped open,
the three of us laughing at the senior men,
the great names, the published ones,
arriving with drawings from another planet,
impossible tolerances, geometry from a dream,
wanting it in their hands by Thursday.
We had names for those drawings.
Inside jokes stacked five years deep.

And we joked the way men joke
in a room with a door and no supervisor.
Nothing was sacred. Everything was material.
I gave as good as i got.
I was the one who said i would die first
in the horror movie, the way we always die,
first reel, no name, no funeral,
and they laughed, and i laughed,
and the joke was mine, i had made it, i owned it,
which is what a young man tells himself
while he is paying to belong
in small pieces of his own skin,
handed over cheerfully, one at a time.

And they were kind, which is the part
that makes the rest of this hard to carry.
They read my face.
They knew before i did when the doubt was climbing,
when a young man was in over his head,
and they would say the right small thing
and send me back to my bench steadier.
I met a wife. I sat at a table.
One of them was among my closest friends
in that whole organization.
Four of us, with a name we called ourselves.
He was one of the four.

Then one night, the three of us and a bottle,
waiting out an experiment,
whiskey presenting itself
the way temptation always does, casually.
Come sit with us. Come laugh with us.
You work too much. We know you love the physics,
but come, sit.
And i sat, and we laughed,
three men warmed by work and company,
and the wound came dressed as laughter.

I did not hear it the first time.
Not because it was quiet.
Because the ear refuses, the way a hand refuses fire.
Something crossed that room and my head filled
with ringing, the way a bell fills with its own sound,
and the words arrived afterward,
out of order, like debris.

You know you are the token black guy here, right?

Meaning, you are here for your skin.
Meaning, someone needed a number and you were the number.
Not the mind. Not the hands.
Not the systems the whole floor runs on.
This.

And this is why i could not name it for a night and a day.
We had said everything to each other in that shop.
I had made the same shape of joke myself,
a hundred times, and been the one laughing loudest.
So which laughter was the door
and which was the hinge coming loose?
You cannot always tell from inside the room.

I went home and asked my brother
the question that shames me now to repeat.
Am i being sensitive, or was that racism?
As if the knife should be consulted
about the depth of the cut.

And the next morning the veil was gone.
I walked into that facility and counted,
for the first time in my life i counted,
and the number came back one.
One, in the entire building.
Me.

And doubt, which had never once
been offered a chair in my fortress,
walked in and sat down in confidence's seat,
asking its patient poison questions.
Was any of it real. The praise, the trust,
the systems, the late nights, the belonging.
Or was i furniture for a photograph,
a number for a form.

There was another day, in the shop.
Metal and laughter and honest work,
and we spoke of the dream itself,
what it would mean if the mission landed,
the years of labor crowned at last.
You are the speaker, they said, and it was true.
The gift of speech my father passed me like land.
You will be the one they interview.
And then the oldest, filthiest word
this country keeps in its cellar
was fixed to my voice, to my father's gift,
and offered to the room as comedy.

I did not laugh.
I know what that word is.
I came here from somewhere else
and i still know exactly what it is,
what it was built for, what it was used for,
whose mouths carried it and over whose bodies.
The ringing came back, the same ringing,
the bell filling with its own sound.

And i understood what the laughing was.
Not a joke that went too far.
A hand set on a door
to learn how far the door will open.

I will not write the word.
It has had enough hands carry it.

There was a senior man in that room,
and he did not laugh either.
He walked out and went straight
to the highest rooms of that company,
and my mentor was already on his way.

Two men heard it and did not laugh.
That is why i am able to tell this
as a story with an ending
instead of a thing i carry alone.

Then it moved without me.
The chief walked down to the shop himself
and told them to go home, they would be contacted.
A lawyer came. There were interviews.
I answered the questions.

And what i felt, i have never said aloud.
Numb.
Not vindicated. Not avenged. Numb,
the way you watch weather
happen to a house you live in.

The investigation opened doors
i had not known were doors.
He had made a joke about gas chambers.
There was a Jewish man in the room.
After that, what he said to me
stops being a mystery
and becomes a pattern.

And the women.
What he did to the women he did
where nobody was standing,
which is the entire craft of it.
Words with a hand inside them.
A room a woman leaves
carrying something she cannot prove.
They had been carrying it for years.
It took a lawyer in the building
before it was safe to set down.

So i was not the first. I was the last,
the case that finally cracked the floor.
Somewhere down that hallway a woman
had been counting far longer than i had,
and no one had come running for her.

I broke, eventually.
I told my mentor i was starting to believe it,
that the word had found a foothold in me,
and he looked at me with such sadness.
And the house that had held my wounding
turned and paid for my mending.
Therapy, offered plainly, no shame attached.
Weeks off, anywhere in the country.
I flew to sit near him for a few days
until i could say the true word out loud.
Racism. Not sensitivity. The other thing.
Not every house does this. Mine did.

Then i came back, and the shop was empty.

The lathe still there. The mill still there.
The work still needing hands.
So i did it myself. I set my own stock,
held my own tolerances, ran my own finishes,
and every single thing i knew how to do in that room
had been taught to me
by the men who were no longer allowed inside it.

I used their gift to fill their absence.
There is no clean word for that.

And guilt came, uninvited, and stayed a while.
Should i have softened it in the interview.
Should i have called it nothing.
Should i have been angrier than i was.
Two men lost their living,
and one of them had been my friend.

Because they were not one man.
One had been doing this for years,
in every corner of that building,
to whoever was smallest in the room.
The other never started anything.
He only stood beside it and laughed along
and could not find the moment to interrupt,
and that is how a good man is lost.
Not for the thing he did.
For the thing he would not stop.

Two nights later, somebody knocked at my door.

It was my friend. He had driven to my house.
I opened it and he put his arms around me
and i put my arms around him,
and he apologized with his whole face,
tears and all, saying he did not mean it,
he did not mean it, he was sorry.

We are still friends today.

I have stopped trying to explain that
to people who want this story simple.

I once wrote that the gifts of my hand,
the gifts of my mind, could never be shaken.
That was not true.
They were shaken. Badly. For a long season.
Other people had to help me set them back on the shelf,
and i have never forgotten which people.

What remains is smaller than triumph
and more durable.
I still love that lab. When i think of those years
i think first of the music, the traces, the mission,
the three of us laughing at an impossible drawing.
The other thing is a scar and behaves like one.
Quiet. Present. Mine.

But i notice everything now.
A child who finds the thing
he was not meant to find
cannot give it back.
He grows up and walks into good rooms,
loves the people in them,
laughs at their jokes and means it,

and counts.
