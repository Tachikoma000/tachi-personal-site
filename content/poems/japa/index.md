They say a hen that hatches ducklings
must learn to watch them from the bank.
My country hatched us. My country watched.
Nobody has written down what it cost her.

Japa. The word is Yoruba. To run.
To bolt. To be gone before the door swings back.
It used to be a word for a thief in a market.
Now it is a word for a graduating class.

Count what she spent on us. Count it honestly.

Mothers who sold what they had to sell,
and then sold the thing they had said they would never sell.
Fathers on night buses with the fare and nothing after.
Teachers who came through the flood and the strike
and taught anyway, in a room with no fan,
forty children to a room, and taught anyway.

We did our homework by candle and kerosene
because NEPA took the light,
as NEPA always took the light,
a whole country bent over its books by flame,
and in the yards the generators coughing,
and the small cheap ones, the ones a family saves for,
carrying the name the market gave them.
I better pass my neighbour.
Go and find me a truer sentence
about what we were doing to each other
while the state did nothing to either of us.

Then WAEC. Then JAMB. Then the waiting.
And when the lecturers struck, as they struck,
a year would go missing out of a young life,
and no one was compensated for the year,
and the year did not come back.

And still she raised doctors out of that.
Engineers. Nurses. Pharmacists. Physicists.
Out of the candle and the flood and the missing year,
she raised them anyway,
which is the part the world never puts in the report.

Then, before first light, the queue outside the embassy.
Grown men with their folders held against the chest
like something that might try to escape.
Nurses who have delivered a hundred children
standing in the cold to be asked
whether they intend to return.
A country's best, in a line, on the pavement,
outside a building that is not in their country,
waiting for a stranger behind glass
to decide the rest of their lives before lunch.

And then Murtala Muhammed, the departure hall,
which is the true national cathedral,
where every family in Nigeria has knelt.
Mothers at the barrier praying over sons
with both hands raised and the whole terminal watching.
Go. Go and do well. Do not look back.
The blessing and the wound arriving in one breath,
because she is asking him to leave her,
and she means it,
and she will cry in the car park after,
and she will not tell him.

Now count what arrived on the other side.

Wards in England running through the night
on Nigerian hands.
Some grandmother in Leeds at three in the morning
being told in a Lagos accent that she will be fine,
and believing it, and being right.
Laboratories in America. Rigs in the Gulf.
Papers in journals with our names on them
and somebody else's city underneath.
My own brother drew the chambers of the heart
as a boy in a compound with no light.
He mends them now in a country
that did not pay for a single one of his lessons.

And now count what stayed behind.

A ward in a town with a name you do not know.
In the ward, a woman.
In the woman, a thing that could have been caught early.
No doctor. Not because none was trained.
Because the one who was trained is in Manchester,
and the one after him is in Toronto,
and the one after that is studying for the exam
that will take him to Riyadh in the spring.

She was not killed by anybody.
That is the whole difficulty.
There is no man to name.
Only a long arithmetic performed on her
by people who never met her
and did not intend her any harm.

And do not make saints of us who went.
And do not make fools of the ones who stayed.

They are holding the roof up.
Teaching in the room with no fan.
Running a clinic on a generator and a promise.
Building the thing anyway, in the country, with the country,
while the rest of us send money
and call it presence.

Put me in the ledger too. I belong in it.
Fifteen years old at the barrier with my mother,
and everything since then paid for
by a place that will not see the interest.
I count rooms now, in other people's countries,
looking for a face like mine and rarely finding one,
and it has never once occurred to me
that i am also the reason
a room somewhere behind me is empty.

I am the harvest of a farm i do not farm.

Somewhere tonight the light will come back
to a compound in Lagos,
and the old shout will go up, up NEPA,
the way it has gone up in that yard for fifty years.

Listen to how thin it is now.

Half the voices that used to make that noise
are making other noises in other countries,
in other accents, at other hours,
into telephones held against the ear of a woman
who says she is fine, she is managing,
the light is on today, thank God,
come home when you can.

We are coming home.
We have been coming home for twenty years.
